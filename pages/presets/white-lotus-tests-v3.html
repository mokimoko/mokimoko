Starting Message

The Lowmarket smelled of yesterday's cod and the vinegar used to wash the slate stalls. Edmund Relfthorne stood near the eastern arcade, his back pressed against sun-warmed brick, wearing a coat of burgundy broadcloth that had cost enough to feed a dockworker's family for two months. The fabric pulled across his shoulders; he had not had it tailored since his nineteenth birthday, before the sedentary softness had settled over his frame. A patchy beard hid his weak chin imperfectly, and his pale grey eyes—bloodshot from last night's brandy and this morning's self-loathing—tracked the movement of a grubboy near the tea merchant's cart.

Ned had been watching the child for twenty minutes. The boy, perhaps eleven, with the smallpox scars that marked him as a survivor of the same epidemic that had kissed Ned's left temple, moved with the jerky caution of prey animals. Ned had decided this meant guilt. His fingers tightened around the whalebone handle of his walking stick, which he had purchased three weeks ago for the falconry he had already abandoned. This time, he thought, Mother will have to acknowledge the initiative.

He pushed off from the wall. His boots, poorly polished, scattered sawdust as he crossed the gap between the arcade and the cart. The grubboy saw him coming and froze, clutching a bundle of linen to his chest.

"You," Ned said. His voice cracked slightly—he had not spoken to anyone since shouting at his valet that morning. He cleared his throat and raised the stick, angling it like a conductor's baton. "Open that parcel."

The boy's mouth worked. Around them, the market continued its hemorrhage of noise—once, a cartwheel squealed against the cobbles—but faces turned with the slow inevitability of a crowd scenting diverting catastrophe.

"I haven't done nothing," the boy said.

"Grammar," Ned snapped, stepping closer. He could smell the child now, the honest stink of sweat and lanolin, and it offended him precisely because it was inescapable, because it rooted them both in this place where Ned did not belong but desperately needed to conquer. "You haven't done anything. And I say you have. The watchmen will sort the particulars."

He reached for the boy's collar with his left hand, the one with the surprising grip strength developed from years of clutching reins, doorframes, and the arms of chairs while his mother delivered her assessments of his character. He did not see the knife until the blade, rust-pitted and small, appeared in the boy's fist with the desperate competence of someone who had been cornered before.

Ned's breath stopped. He realized, with the clarity of a man falling down a well, that he had not thought past the apprehension. That he had no rope, no ally, no plan for resistance.

"Let go," the boy whispered.

Ned did not let go.

Notes

I still really like the concept of the Variety Toggle. It's not so wild that it completely derails the roleplay, but it encourages output that isn't quite as predictable. Overall, I think the writing style is pretty good across all of the models; there's a bit of slop but not as much as there could be. GLM and Deepseek family models are more prone to this, or it could be that I'm more primed to be annoyed by their brand of slop.

I tried with a Default preset—a very simple "Respond as {{char}}" type prompt—with GLM 5 Thinking just to compare. In White Lotus' GLM 5 Thinking responses, Ned starts worrying about what his mother's reaction will be when she hears, and in the second, he's determined to get his hands on User and the kid. In three consecutive swipes with a basic preset, he just doesn't really do anything. He either slinks away, or stands there doing literally nothing lol.

But that was and is my continuing goal: to prompt in such a way that a plot develops, and STUFF happens. That's what makes RP so fun in my opinion—the unexpected, the thrill of being surprised. Anyway, that's it for this round of tests!
